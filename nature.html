Nature & Joy

Why the outdoors resets me, and what psychology says about the little things that make us happy.

Photo: mine, Iceland
Why I love it

Two things get me there: swimming and hiking.

Being outside is where I slow down. It's fresh air and a clear head after a long day of reading and writing, and time that isn't scheduled, measured, or on a screen.

Swimming, just because

I don't swim for laps or a workout. I swim because it feels good: floating, drifting, being in the water with nowhere to be. It's one of the few times my mind goes quiet.

Hiking for the view

I hike for the payoff: the overlook, the waterfall, the moment the trees open up and you can see for miles. Some of the most beautiful places are only reachable on foot, and that makes them feel earned.

The psychology of joy

Why certain things reliably make us happy

I've always been curious about why certain things bring us joy. It turns out researchers have spent a lot of time on that question, and a few ideas really stuck with me.

E. O. Wilson

Biophilia

Humans may have an innate pull toward living things and natural places, a leftover from when survival depended on reading the landscape.

Rachel & Stephen Kaplan

Attention restoration

Focused work drains our "directed attention." Nature holds attention gently (soft fascination), which lets that mental muscle recover.

Roger Ulrich

Stress recovery

His research, including a famous study of hospital patients with a window view of trees, suggests natural scenes lower stress and speed recovery.

Dacher Keltner

Awe

Being near something vast shrinks our sense of self in a good way, so worries feel smaller and connection feels bigger. It's exactly what I feel at the top of a good hike.

Blue‑space research

Blue spaces

Time near or in water (lakes, rivers, the ocean) is linked to lower stress and a calmer mood, which may be why an easy swim feels so restorative.

Happiness research

Savoring & small joys

We adapt fast to big changes, but small, repeated pleasures keep landing, especially when we pause to really take them in.
